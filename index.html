About Me

I'm an assistant professor of economics at New York University's Stern School of Business. I'm a macroeconomist working on public finance, development, and political economy. My research covers four themes: taxation and social insurance, state capacity in Africa, sovereign debt and global finance, and incentives and market design, from performance pay to fee mechanisms for blockchains.

  • Theme Leader, The Role of the Public Sector, STEG
  • Research Fellow, CEPR, CESifo, and HCEO
  • Fellow, Royal Economic Society, Finance for Development Lab, AUL, and BWC

I received my PhD in economics from Northwestern University. Before that I studied at École Polytechnique and Lycée Louis-le-Grand in Paris, after growing up in Senegal. In 2026 I received the NextGen Best African Economist Under 40 Award from the African Development Bank, The Africa Report, and Jeune Afrique, and was elected a Fellow of the Royal Economic Society.

Flexible Retirement and Optimal Taxation

(with Zhixiu Yu)

Conditionally accepted, Quarterly Journal of Economics. September 2026.

How should retirement benefits, labor taxes, and savings incentives be designed when health declines with age? We build a life-cycle model with health shocks, wage risk, and endogenous retirement, and estimate it on US microdata. Health affects retirement through fixed costs of working, lower wages, and higher mortality. Optimal labor wedges are hump-shaped over the life cycle and lower for workers in poor health. Mortality risk can make it optimal to subsidize saving, especially for older and less healthy workers. Retirement consumption should rise more steeply with claiming age than under the current delayed retirement credit. Flexible policies that reward later retirement and account for health come closer to the efficient allocation than a uniform increase in the retirement age.
SSRN. BibTeX. X summary. Project website. Coverage: Chicago Fed Letter.

Property Taxes and Housing Allocation Under Financial Constraints

(with Joshua Coven, Sebastian Golder, and Arpit Gupta)

Revise and resubmit, American Economic Review. August 2026. 2026 Yuki Arai Research Prize.

Low property taxes in California keep elderly owners in their homes and limit housing access for younger families. Raising property taxes reallocates housing toward the young through two channels: lower purchase prices reduce down payments for financially constrained buyers, and higher annual bills raise the cost of holding a home for long-tenured owners. In an overlapping generations model, raising California's rates to Texas levels increases homeownership among younger households and reduces it among older ones. Removing the step-up in basis at death also lowers elderly ownership, so part of older owners' tenure reflects the tax advantages of bequeathing property. The tax treatment of housing shapes how it is allocated across generations.
SSRN. BibTeX. X summary. Video. Coverage: Bloomberg, Minneapolis Fed, Slate, NYU Stern, TaxProf Blog, CoStar.

Blockchain Fee Policies: Quantity- vs Price-Control Design across Protocols

Revise and resubmit, Management Science. July 2026.

Blockchains choose between two fee policies: Bitcoin fixes block size and lets fees clear through bidding, while Ethereum sets a base fee and lets block size adjust. I model validators as temporary monopolists who bargain with the protocol designer. Price controls perform better when demand is volatile, costs are weakly correlated with demand, and validators have strong bargaining power. Quantity controls perform better when costs rise with demand and validators have little influence. Ethereum's base fee adjusts faster than is optimal, which can make fees and block sizes oscillate. I propose bounds on block size targets that limit exploitation of maximal extractable value.
SSRN. BibTeX. Coverage: a16z Crypto Research.

A Theory of International Boycotts

Conditionally accepted, Journal of International Economics. June 2026.

I develop a neoclassical trade model of international boycotts in which a coalition of domestic households maximizes its welfare while holding foreign welfare at a tolerated level. The coalition acts like atomistic, price-taking consumers who each face fixed budgets for spending on each country's goods. Optimal boycotts take the form of outright bans on importing certain foreign goods, and boycotters may work more when income effects dominate substitution effects. A full domestic boycott is equivalent to targeted import tariffs or a voluntary export restraint by foreign firms. Households outside the boycott leak imports and weaken its effect.
SSRN. BibTeX. X summary. Coverage: NPR Marketplace, La Tribune.

Published & Accepted

Bonus Question: How Does Flexible Incentive Pay Affect Unemployment Dynamics?

(with Meghana Gaur, John Grigsby, and Jonathon Hazell)

Forthcoming, American Economic Review: Insights, Vol. 8, No. 4, December 2026.

We introduce dynamic incentive contracts into a model of unemployment fluctuations. Our main result is that wage cyclicality from incentives does not affect the response of unemployment to productivity shocks. The response of unemployment is the same, to a first-order, in two economies: one with flexible incentive pay, and another with exogenously fixed wages. This equivalence is due to movements in effort. Under the optimal incentive contract, firms' profits do not change when wages fall, because the effort of the worker falls too.
NBER. BibTeX. X summary. Coverage: Marginal Revolution, NYU Stern, Atlanta Fed.

How to Fund Unemployment Insurance with Informality and False Claims: Evidence from Senegal

(with Kyle Herkenhoff, Abdoulaye Cisse, Alessandro Dell'Acqua, and Ahmadou A. Mbaye)

Journal of Monetary Economics, vol. 150, March 2025, 103699.

We study the welfare effects of unemployment insurance in Senegal, where formal workers are a small share of the labor force and informal workers can file false claims. We build a model with varying enforcement and funding sources and estimate it with a custom labor force survey. Liquidity gains are large and moral hazard responses are small: an additional dollar of benefits yields a consumption-equivalent gain of 60 to 90 cents, several times larger than comparable US estimates. Payroll-tax funding delivers the largest gains but becomes infeasible when few workers are formal and false claims are common. Consumption-tax funding delivers smaller gains but remains feasible under high informality.
Journal article. BibTeX. Data. X summary. Coverage: VoxDev, Brookings, World Bank.

Redistribution with Performance Pay

(with Pawel Doligalski and Nicolas Werquin)

Journal of Political Economy Macroeconomics, vol. 1, no. 2, pp. 371–402, June 2023.

Half of the jobs in the United States feature pay for performance. We derive incidence and optimum formulas for the rate of tax progressivity and the top income tax rate when such labor contracts arise from moral hazard frictions within firms. Our first main result is that the sensitivity of the worker's compensation to performance is roughly invariant to tax progressivity. Second, the optimal tax schedule is strictly less progressive than in standard models that treat pretax earnings risk as exogenous. Quantitatively, the welfare cost of not accounting for performance pay when choosing tax progressivity is 0.3% of consumption.
Journal article. BibTeX. X summary. Coverage: Minneapolis Fed.

Optimal Default in a Small Open Economy: Senegal's Hidden Debt Crisis

(with Louphou Coulibaly)

Oxford Review of Economic Policy, vol. 42, issue 2, Summer 2026, pp. 362–376, October 2026.

Hidden public debt can abruptly reprice sovereign risk once it is revealed. We model optimal default after such a revelation and apply the framework to Senegal's 2024 debt audit, which sharply raised reported debt. A baseline calibration implies that default would have been optimal from 2023 onward. Because Senegal has kept repaying, we also build a no-default calibration. It requires income shocks more volatile and less persistent than history suggests, and places Senegal near the default boundary, where an adverse shock of about 3 percent could make restructuring optimal within a year. The revelation moved Senegal from moderate fiscal risk to acute vulnerability.
Journal article. SSRN.

Parallel Execution Fee Mechanisms

Financial Cryptography and Data Security (FC 2025), February 2026, pp. 245–262.

How can prices allocate resources efficiently in blockchains that process transactions through several parallel queues sharing one global capacity limit? Revenue-maximizing pricing tends to give all capacity to the highest-paying queue, while welfare-maximizing pricing generally serves every queue. Optimal relative prices depend on market size, demand elasticity, and local versus global congestion. The results apply to parallel-execution blockchains, DAG-based systems, and designs with multiple concurrent proposers.
Proceedings. BibTeX. Coverage: a16z Crypto Research.

Blockchain Price vs. Quantity Controls

Financial Cryptography and Data Security (FC 2024), February 2025, pp. 303–313.

How should blockchains set transaction fees? I compare price-based controls, such as Ethereum's EIP-1559 base fee, with quantity-based controls, such as a fixed block size limit. Demand uncertainty, validator costs, cryptocurrency price swings, demand elasticity, and decentralization all shape which approach performs better. Modeling the choice as Nash bargaining between protocol designers and validators, I show that no single mechanism fits every blockchain, and hybrid designs combining both controls can be useful.
Proceedings. BibTeX.

NewTax Productivity and Development: A Quantitative Macroeconomic Analysis

(with Radhika Goyal, Andres Jensen, David Lagakos, and Yuntian Zhao)

Submitted. October 2026.

Does governments' ability to collect taxes drive economic development? Using a new dataset, we show that tax revenue per dollar spent on collection rises steeply with income. We build a growth model in which tax collection uses labor and revenue funds productive public capital. A 50 percent rise in tax collection productivity raises long-run output in low-income economies far more than standard growth accounting predicts, with smaller gains in richer countries.

PreliminaryEmployer Market Power with Endogenous Labor Market Boundaries

(with Olalekan Bello and Claudia Macaluso)

May 2026.

Studies of employer market power usually take labor market boundaries as fixed, such as commuting zones or counties. Using more than 63 million online applications to US hourly jobs, we show that search breadth varies widely across workers and rises when local labor markets weaken: workers search farther when their home market is concentrated or cyclically weak. We build a search-and-matching model with granular firms and costly search across distance, and confirm its four predictions for applications and wages. Employer market power depends jointly on local concentration and on how workers reallocate their search.

Selected Ongoing Research

Public Service Access and Fiscal Capacity

(with Hamidou Jawara, Justine Knebelmann, Joseph Levine, and Victor Pouliquen)

Analysis completed. Kanifing, The Gambia. J-PAL DigiFi grant.

Cities in developing countries face growing demand for public services but raise too little tax revenue to fund them. Can local governments harness synergies between the two? We partner with The Gambia's largest urban municipality to implement six randomized interventions targeting property taxation and trash collection, a service households can access by paying a user fee. At baseline, only 34% of households pay property taxes, while public trucks collect just 30% of waste. We develop a model of households' joint decisions to pay property taxes and service fees to guide our analysis. Strong enforcement through court summons sharply increases tax compliance, while comprehensive enforcement using GIS technology has smaller but meaningful effects. A tax cut has no effect. Improving access to trash collection and providing information about the tax-service link do not increase compliance. By contrast, a reciprocity intervention that reduces user fees conditional on tax payment raises compliance by a similar magnitude to comprehensive enforcement. Tying user fees to tax payments is thus a promising way to sustain a virtuous cycle.
